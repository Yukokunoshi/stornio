Statutory warranty (legal guarantee) vs. commercial warranty in the EU

In the EU, two different things are called a guarantee or warranty. One is your legal right, the other is an extra promise. Mixing them up is the most common reason people give up on a repair they were entitled to.

4 min read · Updated 2026-11-20

General information, not legal advice. National rules can differ and can change; check your country's official consumer information or a consumer advice body before you act.

The legal guarantee

Under the Sale of Goods Directive (2019/771), the seller is responsible for any lack of conformity that exists when the goods are delivered and becomes apparent within at least two years. Countries may set a longer period. It is free and you have it automatically, whatever the seller's own policy says.

If a fault appears within one year of delivery, it is presumed to have existed at delivery, so you do not have to prove that. Some countries extend this to two years.

For second-hand goods, countries may allow the seller and the buyer to agree on a shorter period, but not shorter than one year.

What you can ask for

First you can choose between repair and replacement, free of charge and within a reasonable time, unless your choice is impossible or disproportionate for the seller. If the seller refuses, the repair or replacement fails, or the fault is serious, you can ask for a price reduction or end the contract and get your money back.

Your claim under the legal guarantee is against the seller, not the manufacturer.

The commercial warranty

A commercial warranty (often called a manufacturer's warranty or extended warranty) is a voluntary promise by the manufacturer or the seller. Its length and conditions are whatever the warranty statement says. It comes on top of the legal guarantee and cannot reduce your legal rights.

The warranty statement must say that you also have the free legal remedies against the seller.

What to do when something breaks

  • Find the receipt or another proof of purchase and the delivery date.
  • Describe the fault in writing, with photos if useful.
  • Contact the seller under the legal guarantee, or the warranty provider under the commercial warranty, whichever suits you better.
  • Keep copies of everything you send and receive.

Common questions

Is the legal guarantee in the EU two years?

At least two years from delivery for new goods. Some countries give longer periods.

The manufacturer's warranty expired after one year. Do I still have rights?

Yes, if the legal guarantee period has not ended. The legal guarantee runs separately and is the seller's responsibility.

Do I have to pay for the repair under the legal guarantee?

No. Repair or replacement under the legal guarantee must be free of charge, including the cost of sending the goods back.
